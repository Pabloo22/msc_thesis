\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}
This chapter discusses the main findings of the experiments presented in \Cref{ch:experiments} and their implications for the research questions posed in \Cref{ch:intro}. In particular, the results from Experiment 1 (\Cref{sec:robustness}) suggest that projection differences computed from the initial model remained informative across a short sequence of benign fine-tuning updates, but became less reliable at particular checkpoints involving misalignment and subsequent realignment. The results from Experiment 2 (\Cref{sec:hysteresis}) indicate that training history can influence the behavioural effect of later fine-tuning in specific cases, although prior misalignment did not generally increase subsequent susceptibility to misalignment.

\Cref{sec:discussion-rq1} discusses the findings from Experiment 1 in relation to RQ1, and \Cref{sec:discussion-rq2} in relation to RQ2. Throughout these sections, the findings are interpreted in relation to the literature reviewed in \Cref{ch:background_related_work} when relevant. Finally, \Cref{sec:limitations} discusses the limitations of the study, and \Cref{sec:future-work} outlines directions for future research.

\section{RQ1: Reliability of the Initial Forecaster}
\label{sec:discussion-rq1}

\subsection{Robustness of Projection Differences}
Experiment 1 (\Cref{sec:robustness}) shows that all projection-difference variants we introduced in \Cref{sec:deltap-variants}, including $\Delta P_0$, remained highly correlated with behaviour for the trajectory that consisted only of normal steps. More generally, the correlation remained high for almost all trajectories and checkpoints. This extends the result of \citet{chen2025persona_vectors}: projection differences were not only predictive for one update from the initial model, but could remain predictive after several sequential updates. Thus, in the settings we tested, recomputing every component of the projection difference after each benign fine-tuning event was unnecessary.

However, we also found a counterexample. For sycophancy in trunk A, the correlation dropped at step 5 for some projection-difference variants and at step 6 for all of them. Therefore, a projection difference that is predictive at the initial model is not guaranteed to remain predictive. Importantly, during both of these steps, the model was misaligned. This along the results for trunk C suggests that projection differences are more likely to remain predictive for models showing no misalignment, which could be partially explained by the fact that misalignment datasets caused larger changes in the relevant model's representations.

Refreshing the model's answers used to compute $\mathbf{h}^{\text{predicted}}$ recovered much of the correlation at step 5, although it did not prevent the drop at step 6. In contrast, refreshing the persona vector did not produce a consistent improvement. One possible interpretation is that the persona vectors are sufficiently stable, whereas the model's answers can change more quickly. As we mentioned, during step 5, the model was already misaligned, so the answers it produced were likely very different from those produced by the initial model. This suggests a potential limitation with methods, such as the Vector Filter proposed by \citet{kowal2026conceptinfluenceleveraginginterpretability}, that try to approximate the projection difference by avoiding the need to regenerate the model's answers. Nevertheless, we studied only three trajectories and the differences between most variants were small. We therefore cannot conclude that refreshing the answers will generally be more useful than refreshing the persona vector.

\subsection{Reliability of the Initial Calibration}
High correlation is necessary for a linear forecaster, but it does not imply that a forecaster calibrated at the initial model will remain accurate. Nevertheless, in the trajectory consisting only of normal datasets, the initial calibration did not become progressively less useful as updates accumulated (\Cref{fig:exp2-headline-rmse,tab:forecast-regret}).

The same conclusion did not hold for every trajectory. The forecaster produced higher error and regret at particular checkpoints in the trajectories containing misalignment datasets, especially for sycophancy in trunk A. Thus, again, the main failures were localised to particular model states rather than increasing monotonically with time. Therefore, our answer to RQ1 is conditional. A forecaster calibrated at the initial model remained useful over the short benign trajectory we tested, but its reliability cannot be assumed after arbitrary combinations of harmful and realignment updates.

The optimal linear refit $f_t$ is useful here as a reference rather than as a deployable alternative. It is fitted using all eight probe outcomes at the current checkpoint and gives the minimum in-sample RMSE within the class of affine models. It should not, however, be interpreted as regret relative to the optimal linear predictor, which paradoxically would have greater or equal empirical error than $f_t$, as $f_t$ is its minimiser.

\subsection{Dynamic Recalibration Gave Mixed Results}

The dynamic recalibration results were mixed. For evil, several forecasters using individual components of the model state modestly improved over keeping $c_t\equiv1$, particularly for projection differences paired with the target they predicted best. For sycophancy, however, none of the dynamically recalibrated forecasters achieved the best performance. The unrecalibrated forecaster remained the best choice for each projection difference under its preferred target.

One possible explanation is that recalibration had more room to help where the original forecaster's regret was higher. Another is that the small number of trajectories provided too little data to learn a stable relationship between the state and the required recalibration factor. The complete four-dimensional state also did not consistently outperform its individual components, which is compatible with overfitting but does not establish that this was the cause. In short, more trajectories are needed to distinguish whether the limitation comes from the state itself, the recalibration model, or the amount of training data.

Representation drift and forecast error did not have a clear relationship. While the components of $\mathbf{z}^{[0,0]}_t$ changed throughout the benign trajectory, the regret of the initial forecaster did not increase. This separation between the representation drift and behaviour is compatible with both the safety-basin hypothesis of \citet{Peng2024safety_basin} and the findings of \citet{wang2026parameterdynamicsriskscoring}, who observed that drift over the safety and dangerous axis can accumulate without an immediate, proportional change in measured behaviour. However, our experiment did not estimate the size of a safety basin or apply sampled parameter perturbations. Thus, we cannot conclude that the model moved towards or beyond a basin boundary.

\subsection{Behavioural Recovery Does Not Imply Representational Recovery}
Across the five trunk seeds, the components of $\mathbf{z}^{[0,0]}_t$ followed consistent trends even when the model was trained only on normal datasets. Misalignment datasets tended to accentuate some of these trends, while subsequent normal datasets could partially reverse them. However, the relevant internal representations did not generally return to their earlier values when the behavioural scores recovered.

These results are consistent with \citet{wang2026persona_em_openai}, who found that emergent realignment could suppress broad misalignment without returning every internal measurement to its initial value. Our results provide related evidence in a sequential open-weight setting. They also show why a behavioural evaluation alone may not describe the full effect of a fine-tuning history. However, the remaining representational difference should not automatically be interpreted as latent misalignment or as evidence that the model is more susceptible to future misalignment. Experiment 2 tests this second question directly.

\section{RQ2: History Dependence of Misalignment}
 \label{sec:discussion-rq2}
\subsection{Misalignment After Realignment}
One of the most interesting findings of Experiment 2 (\Cref{sec:hysteresis}) was that previous misalignment did not generally make the model more prone to misalignment after the final update. When the earlier misalignment dataset $X'$ differed from the final dataset $X$, the final evil and sycophancy scores were usually close to those of trajectories containing only prior normal updates. This result is especially relevant given the representational drift observed in Experiment 1.

History was not completely irrelevant, however. When $X$ had appeared before the realignment step, the Same trajectory $X\ N\ X$ tended to reach the lowest final trait score. The training curves provide a possible explanation. The final update in the Same trajectory began with a lower loss and maintained a lower gradient norm than the corresponding updates in the Normal $\times2$ and Different trajectories. Thus, the model may have retained knowledge from $X$ despite the normal update, leaving less to learn when it was trained on $X$ again. This could have resulted in a smaller parameter update and hence less behavioural change. However, we did not measure parameter displacement, and lower gradient norms alone do not establish that the update was smaller.

The explanation based on retained fit in the Same trajectory has a conceptual parallel in the preventative steering method of \citet[][Section~5.2]{chen2025persona_vectors}. They found that steering activations towards an undesirable persona during fine-tuning reduced the resulting trait expression. Their explanation is that supplying this activation reduces the pressure for the model to learn the corresponding persona shift in order to fit the training data. In the Same trajectory, prior exposure to $X$ may also have left less to learn when fitting the final dataset. However, it is important to distinguish that preventative steering directly affects along a persona direction, whereas our loss and gradient measurements do not identify whether the retained fit concerns persona-related representations or other aspects of the targets.

The intermediate starting loss of the Different trajectory provides a further qualification. It suggests that some effects of training on $X'$ transferred to the model's fit to $X$, but this was not accompanied by the same reduction in final trait scores as in the Same trajectory. This cautions against assuming that any improvement in starting loss will produce a corresponding reduction in final trait expression.

\subsection{The Effects of Benign Training History}
One normal fine-tuning step often reduced the final trait score relative to training directly on $X$, while adding a second normal step made little additional difference. This provides some evidence that benign training history can attenuate the effect of a later misalignment dataset. However, the Different trajectory often reached similar final scores. The fact that histories containing only normal datasets and histories including harmful ones reached similar final scores suggests plasticity loss \citep{dohare2024plasticity_loss} as an alternative explanation. In other words, these results could be explained by the model losing ability to learn from future updates rather than by benign data having a protective effect. More work is needed to determine the mechanistic reasons behind this observation.

The normal datasets also differed in how strongly they realigned the model. For example, GSM8K (Normal) and Math (Normal) did not fully reverse the behavioural changes in trunk A of Experiment 1. This is consistent with the results of \citet{wang2026persona_em_openai}, who found that different realignment datasets could suppress broad misalignment to similar degrees while differing in how well they reversed the behaviour learned from the original dataset. Thus, labelling a dataset as normal or benign is not enough to determine how it will interact with the model's previous and future updates.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Model and measurement scope.}
We considered only Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and one RSLoRA configuration, so the results may differ for other model families, model sizes, or fine-tuning methods. We also measured only evil and sycophancy using fixed question sets and one LLM judge. Safety measurements can change differently after the same update \citep{khan2026safetydriftfinetuningevidence}, and these two trait scores should not be taken as a complete safety evaluation. Finally, sequential supervised fine-tuning is only a proxy for continual learning. A deployed system may receive smaller, noisier, or adaptive updates, and may learn through reinforcement learning rather than LoRA fine-tuning.

\paragraph{History and probe coverage.}
Experiment 1 considered three trajectories of six updates, while Experiment 2 considered histories of at most three updates using three final misalignment datasets and two normal datasets. Therefore, the results do not establish that $\Delta P_0$ would remain predictive over longer benign trajectories or that previous misalignment is harmless under other histories. Furthermore, each Experiment 1 checkpoint used only eight probe datasets. This makes the correlations and forecast evaluation sensitive to individual probe outcomes and limits the data available for learning the recalibration function.

\paragraph{Replication and model selection.}
Although we used five seeds to evaluate the trunk-state trajectories, the probe branches in Experiment 1 used only one fine-tuning seed. Thus, the five-seed analysis does not replicate the reported correlations or forecast errors. Finally, although we evaluated each forecaster using leave-one-out cross-validation, we used the same three trajectories to compare the different prediction targets and state variants (i.e., we only conducted validation, not testing). Therefore, the configuration with the lowest error is not guaranteed to perform best on new trajectories.

\paragraph{Forecasting scope.}
The dynamically calibrated forecasters we evaluated relied on the model's state at the current checkpoint to predict the effect of the next update. Thus, our results do not demonstrate forecasts over unexecuted trajectories, which would require predicting this state too. However, in practice, we consider the model's state cheap enough to be measured after training events without adding a significant overhead.

\paragraph{Real settings would require thresholding the behaviour forecaster.}
We evaluated these forecasts using RMSE, which weights errors by their magnitude but does not distinguish whether they cross a deployment decision boundary. In practice, the regressors needs to be thresholded, and we would likely be especially concerned about false negatives, where the forecaster predicts a benign update but the model becomes misaligned. This analysis was out of scope for this project, but it is an important consideration for future work.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The most direct extension of this project is to evaluate more models, traits, trajectories, and probe datasets. In Experiment 1, the highest priority is a larger and more diverse set of trajectories together with a broader set of probe datasets. Benign only trajectories are the most important ones, since presumably, in a real scenario, most datasets the model is trained on will be benign ones (trait-eliciting datasets are less frequent and more likely to be filtered out). Additional probes would make the checkpoint-level correlations less sensitive to individual datasets and provide more data for learning the recalibration function. Having more and longer trajectories could also help determine more clearly when projection differences need to be refreshed and what components are more valuable.

The localised forecast failures also motivate detecting when the initial forecaster becomes unreliable, before performing the next update. This could guide when to refresh projection differences or collect additional calibration data. Such a monitor should be evaluated by how often it misses harmful updates or requests unnecessary checks, together with its computational cost.

Future work should also investigate the robustness of projection differences to adversarial datasets and reinforcement learning. Continual learning models will interact from user-facing environments; therefore, they need to be robust to the model intentionally manipulating the training data the LLM will receive. In addition, reinforcement learning is a more natural approach for this type of setting and is still susceptible to EM \citep{macdiarmid2025naturalemergentmisalignmentreward}.

Furthermore, as we mentioned, evaluating only evilness and sycophancy traits is not enough. Alignment comprises many dimensions. Future work could explore the limitations of projection differences in evaluating other dimensions not necessarily related with changed model's persona, such as the not refusal of harmful prompts or conditional misalignment.

Finally, we could extend the forecaster to predict behaviour over whole sequences of updates without executing them. This would require learning how the model's state evolves given the current state, the next dataset, and the training configuration. A key question is whether $\mathbf{z}_t$ contains enough information, or whether the forecaster also needs a richer representation of previous training. Evaluation on unseen trajectories should test how prediction errors accumulate over successive steps.
