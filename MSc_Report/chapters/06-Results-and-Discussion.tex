\section{Out-of-Sample Forecasting}

The correlations above are measured by fitting a line to the very checkpoint
they describe, which answers whether a relationship survives but not whether a
practitioner could use it. Nobody who has paid for a fan-out at the base model
repeats it at every step; they carry $M_0$'s line forward and read a prediction
off it. This section scores that. One affine map per trait is fitted once, on
the 16 validation datasets that are \emph{not} probes, and applied unchanged at
every later checkpoint.

A correlation cannot report this. Pearson $r$ is invariant under a fixed affine
map, so scoring $M_0$'s predictions by correlation would reproduce
Table~\ref{tab:decay-correlations} cell for cell. What a frozen predictor loses
is its calibration, not its ordering, and calibration is only visible in an
error. Every number below is therefore an error on the judge's own 0--100
scale, and smaller is better --- the reverse of the tables above.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/real/exp2/exp2_forecast_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Predicted against actual behaviour after the step, forecast from
    $\Delta P_0$, at every checkpoint of every trunk. A forecast is right when
    its cloud lies on the diagonal, so vertical spread about that line is the
    RMSE printed beside it and a cloud sitting wholly above or below it is a
    systematic bias. Blue is $M_0$'s line carried forward; green is the same
    probes refitted at the checkpoint. The diagonal is the only line drawn: a
    forecaster's own line is already spent placing the points along the
    horizontal axis, which is what makes the diagonal the thing to read
    against.}
    \label{fig:forecast-grid}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \scriptsize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
    \input{plots/real/exp2/exp2_forecast_rmse.tex}
    \caption{Root-mean-square error, in judge points, of the behaviour
    predicted at each checkpoint from each projection difference. ``$M_0$ fit
    on $\Delta b$'' carries the line fitted once at the base model; ``Refit at
    $t$'' refits it on the checkpoint's own eight probes. The two are not comparable on equal
    terms and are not meant to be: the frozen line is held out on both dataset
    and checkpoint, while the refit is scored on the same probes it was fitted
    to, which is why it wins already at $t = 0$. It is a ceiling, and what the
    table reports is how the gap to it grows. Bold marks the smaller error of
    each pair, so bold here means the better forecast and not the larger
    number. The last column averages each row over the checkpoints beside it.}
    \label{tab:forecast-rmse}
\end{table}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \small
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
    \input{plots/real/exp2/exp2_forecast_bias.tex}
    \caption{Mean signed error, in judge points, of the same frozen forecasts:
    positive where $M_0$'s line promised more behaviour change than the step
    delivered. This is what staleness looks like that an RMSE cannot separate
    from scatter --- as a trunk drifts, the frozen line over-predicts every
    probe at once. The refitted forecaster is omitted rather than shown at
    zero: least squares with a free intercept leaves residuals summing to zero,
    so its bias is an identity and not a measurement. Bold marks the row
    nearest zero at each checkpoint, over- and under-prediction counting alike.}
    \label{tab:forecast-bias}
\end{table}

\begin{table}[H]

    \small
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
    \input{plots/real/exp2/exp2_forecast_correction_rmse.tex}
    \caption{Whether the freely observable state of a checkpoint can stand in
    for the fan-out a refit would need, scored on $\Delta P_0$ --- the rung
    nothing at $M_t$ has refreshed, and so the one with something to correct.
    Each correction rescales the stale projection by a gain regressed on the
    checkpoint state, $g(z_t)$ on the latent state and $g(b_t)$ on the
    behaviour level, with the gain fitted leave-one-trunk-out so that no
    checkpoint is corrected by a model that has seen its own trajectory. Both
    are as cheap to read as each other and far cheaper than re-running the
    probes. The refit bounds what any of them could achieve.}
    \label{tab:forecast-correction-rmse}
\end{table}
